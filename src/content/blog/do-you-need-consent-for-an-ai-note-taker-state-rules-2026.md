I've had an AI note taker sit in on 200+ meetings by now: client calls, internal syncs, the occasional interview. The tooling is genuinely useful. The consent question is the part almost everyone gets wrong, usually by accident.

Here's the short version, then the detail you need to act on it.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Recording laws change and depend on the facts, so talk to a licensed attorney about your situation.*

## The Short Answer

**Yes, in about 13 states every participant must consent before an AI note taker records: 10 clearly require it, and 3 are disputed, so treat them the same. In the other states, one participant's consent is legally enough. If you don't know where everyone is, get everyone's consent before recording.**

The states to treat as all-party:

- **Clear all-party (10):** California, Connecticut (telephone/electronic), Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Washington
- **Disputed, treat as all-party (3):** Delaware, Michigan, Nevada

Two definitions:

- **One-party consent:** only one person in the conversation (which can be you) needs to agree.
- **All-party consent:** everyone on the call must know and agree before recording starts.

Adding a bot to the calendar invite usually doesn't count as agreement. More on that below.

One caveat on the numbers: state lists disagree. Some sources count 9 all-party states, others 13 or more, and Oregon, for one, splits by conversation type. I explain my count in the table section.

## What Counts as "Consent" for an AI Note Taker, Legally

For years the standard advice was that an AI note taker is just a recording device, like a tape recorder. Courts have generally applied recording laws to these tools that way, and the transcription and summarizing don't change the consent analysis.

That framing is now being tested in court, though. The vendor behind the tool may be treated as its own party, not just an extension of you. I cover that in its own section below.

### The federal floor

The **Wiretap Act (18 U.S.C. § 2511)**, part of the Electronic Communications Privacy Act, sets a one-party consent baseline. If you're a participant, federal law alone doesn't require anyone else's permission.

There's an exception. One-party consent doesn't protect a recording made to commit a crime or a tort (§ 2511(2)(d)). And federal law is a floor, not a ceiling: states can require more, and a dozen or so do.

### One-party vs. all-party, in plain terms

Under one-party consent, a recording is legal as long as one person involved knows about it and agrees. Under all-party consent, everyone on the call has to know and agree before recording starts. Each state picks one, though the details vary (see the table).

### The "reasonable expectation of privacy" test

Some of these laws only apply to private or confidential conversations. California's § 632, for example, turns on whether participants reasonably expected the conversation wasn't being recorded (*Flanagan v. Flanagan*, Cal. 2002). A public webinar with 500 attendees is treated differently than a confidential one-on-one HR conversation. Most work meetings (status updates, client calls, internal syncs) can carry that expectation, so don't assume you're exempt because it's "just a work call."

The disagreements cluster around Delaware, Michigan, and Nevada, and that matters when the answer determines whether your recording is legal.

My approach: 10 states clearly require all-party consent, 3 are genuinely disputed, and I treat all 13 as all-party because the cost of over-complying is a 15-second announcement.

**My practical take:** don't memorize which side of a disputed state you land on. If a meeting touches any state above (or Oregon), treat it as all-party consent required and move on.

## What If Meeting Participants Are in Different States?

This scenario rarely gets a real example, and it trips up remote teams constantly.

The safe rule is: **assume the strictest applicable law controls.** If any participant is in an all-party state, get all-party consent for the whole call, even if you personally are in a one-party state.

**Worked example:** You're in Texas (one-party). A client on the call is in California (all-party). Under Texas law alone, you wouldn't need their permission. But the California Supreme Court held in *Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney* (2006) that California's all-party rule can apply when an out-of-state business records calls with California residents. How a court would treat your specific call depends on the facts, but you shouldn't count on Texas law protecting you. Get explicit consent from everyone.

On most client calls you don't know every participant's location with certainty. In that case, default to all-party consent. It's the safest default.

## Can the AI Vendor Be Liable Too? The "Third-Party Eavesdropper" Question

This is where the law is moving fastest, and it's the biggest gap in most consent guides.

The old assumption is that an AI note taker is a tool in the hands of the person who turned it on, so that person's consent covers it. Plaintiffs are now arguing something different: the vendor is a separate party that captures the audio, stores it on its own servers, and sometimes uses it to train its models. Wiretap laws like the federal Wiretap Act and California's § 631 target third-party eavesdroppers, and one participant's consent may not cover a third party listening in. The vendors' response is that they act at the direction of the meeting host, like a recorder.

### What's happened so far (all allegations, no liability findings)

- **Otter.ai.** In August 2025, Justin Brewer sued in the Northern District of California. He alleged he was recorded on a call by an Otter bot he didn't know about and had no Otter account. Related suits were consolidated as *In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation*, No. 5:25-cv-06911-EKL. On August 13, 2026, the court ruled on Otter's motion to dismiss. Core claims under the federal Wiretap Act, California's privacy law, and Illinois's biometric privacy law survived, on the theory that Otter plausibly acted as a third-party eavesdropper. Other claims, including the computer-intrusion claims, were dismissed, mostly with leave to amend. This is a ruling on the pleadings, not a finding that Otter broke the law, and the case continues. Otter denies wrongdoing.
- **Fireflies.ai.** A class action filed in Illinois in December 2025 alleges the tool creates voiceprints of participants without the consent Illinois's BIPA requires.
- **Granola.** *Chamberlain v. Granola, Inc.* (N.D. Cal., filed July 30, 2026) targets the no-bot model, alleging that silent, invisible capture and training on transcripts by default violate wiretap law.

### What this means for you

1. **Your consent may not be the only one that matters.** If the vendor is treated as a separate party, "I agreed" is a weaker defense than it used to be.
2. **Check whether the tool trains on your recordings, and what the default is.** Non-users on your call never agreed to the vendor's terms. Business plans often let you turn training off, so check yours.
3. **Name the vendor when you disclose.** "This call is being recorded" doesn't tell people that a third-party company is receiving the audio.
4. **Expect this area to keep changing.** Recheck it before you rely on any summary of it, including this one.

## Is Adding an AI Bot to a Calendar Invite Enough to Count as Consent?

Usually not. This is the most common mistake I see teams make.

Adding "Otter.ai Notetaker" or a similar bot to a calendar invite tells people a bot *might* join. It doesn't clearly tell them the call is being recorded, transcribed, and possibly stored indefinitely by a third-party vendor, which is what informed consent requires. Legal commentary generally treats a passive calendar notification as weak evidence of consent in an all-party state. A verbal confirmation at the start of the call is the safer bar.

### Does a Zoom, Teams, or Google Meet notice count?

This is a separate question that people often conflate with AI note taker consent.

The built-in recording banner in Zoom, Teams, or Google Meet covers *that platform's* recording. The same goes for their own AI features (Zoom AI Companion, Teams Copilot, Gemini in Meet). None of these automatically covers a *third-party* note taker that joins separately, captures audio, and sends it to another company's servers. If you run Fireflies or Fathom on top of a Zoom call, the Zoom banner alone doesn't disclose what that tool is doing with the data. Treat them as two separate disclosures.

### What about implied consent?

Some courts recognize implied consent: the participants were clearly told about the recording and stayed on the call anyway. You'll see this argument in legal commentary, and it's why some people say silence is enough.

I wouldn't rely on it. It depends on the facts (was the notice clear, did people have a real chance to object?). Courts differ on it, and California's stricter approach makes it a risky thing to build a workflow on. Silence is weak evidence, and an explicit yes is strong evidence.

### What actually counts as valid consent

- A verbal announcement at the start of the meeting, stated clearly enough that it's captured on the recording itself
- An explicit opt-in, not silence and not "no objections raised"
- Written notice that names the specific AI tool, not just "this call may be recorded"

## Do You Need Consent to Use an AI Note Taker at Work?

Yes. "The company approved this tool" doesn't change the underlying consent law. Company policy can require employees to use an AI note taker. It can't override state wiretapping statutes that require the *other participants'* consent, including participants outside the company.

### Employer wiretapping exposure

If your organization records meetings via AI note taker without proper consent in an all-party state, the legal exposure can sit with the company, not just the employee who clicked "record." I'd treat this as a policy problem, not an individual-judgment problem. Leaving disclosure up to each employee is how companies end up with inconsistent, non-compliant behavior across teams.

### High-stakes meetings: legal, HR, medical

A few contexts deserve extra caution, because the consent question stacks on top of separate confidentiality rules:

- **Legal meetings:** running an AI note taker in a conversation with counsel risks waiving **attorney-client privilege**. If a third-party vendor has access to the transcript, the confidentiality that privilege depends on gets harder to argue.
- **Litigation-adjacent meetings:** recordings and AI-generated summaries prepared in anticipation of litigation may or may not keep **work-product protection**. That protection is fact-specific, not automatic.
- **Medical or health-related meetings:** **HIPAA** requires vetting whether the AI vendor will sign a business associate agreement before it goes near protected health information.
- **Education contexts:** **FERPA** creates similar constraints around meetings involving student records.

If a meeting falls into one of these categories, don't use a general-purpose AI note taker by default. Use one vetted for that compliance context, or don't record at all.

### Sample internal policy line

> "Any AI note taker or transcription tool must be verbally disclosed to all meeting participants before recording begins, regardless of the participants' location. Passive calendar notifications do not satisfy this requirement."

## Does GDPR Require Consent for AI Meeting Transcription?

For meetings involving anyone in the EU, in practice yes, though the legal mechanics differ from US wiretapping law.

GDPR requires a **lawful basis** for processing personal data. Consent is one of several, and it isn't automatically the only one available. But most legal teams treat explicit consent as the cleanest, lowest-risk path for meeting recordings, because the alternatives (legitimate interest, contract necessity) are harder to justify for something as sensitive as a recorded conversation. GDPR also requires you to tell people what you're doing with their data, including who receives it, so a vendor-named disclosure does double duty.

On top of GDPR, individual EU member states have their own criminal codes. Germany's § 201 StGB, for example, criminalizes recording a non-public conversation without consent, which is a separate exposure from GDPR.

**Outside the EU and US:**

- **UK:** UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 work similarly.
- **Canada:** the federal Criminal Code generally allows one-party consent, but privacy laws add notice and consent duties.
- **Australia:** surveillance laws vary by state, and several require the consent of all parties.

**My recommendation:** if any participant is in the EU or another non-US jurisdiction, get explicit, disclosed consent every time, and ask counsel about the specifics.

## How to Actually Ask for Consent

1. **Add disclosure to the calendar invite.** Name the specific tool, not just "this meeting may be recorded."
2. **Announce it verbally when the meeting starts,** before any substantive discussion.
3. **Pause and wait for acknowledgment.** A few seconds of silence isn't agreement.
4. **Offer a real opt-out.** Leaving the call, or asking you to pause the tool, should be genuinely available.
5. **Log consent for high-stakes meetings.** For legal, medical, or HR contexts, note in writing that consent was obtained.

### Calendar invite template

> "This meeting will be recorded and transcribed using [Tool Name] for note-taking purposes. The recording may be shared with [team/stored for X days]. Let me know before the call if you'd prefer it not be used."

### Verbal script for the start of a meeting

> "Quick note before we start: I'm using [Tool Name] to record and transcribe this call so I don't have to take notes manually. Is everyone okay with that?"

That takes about fifteen seconds, and it puts you on the safest footing in every US state. It doesn't replace separate duties under GDPR, BIPA, HIPAA, or privilege rules, but it's the habit that covers the wiretap question.

### If someone declines

Turn the tool off for that call. There's no workaround. Proceeding anyway in an all-party state is exactly the scenario that creates liability.

## What Happens If You Skip Consent

- **Wiretapping liability.** In states like Illinois, Florida, and Massachusetts, unauthorized recording can carry felony charges, not just civil exposure. California adds civil damages of $5,000 per violation or three times actual damages.
- **Vendor liability, and yours.** As covered above, vendors are being sued as alleged third-party eavesdroppers. That doesn't automatically shield the people who turned the tool on.
- **Vendor data access and retention.** Many AI note taker vendors retain transcripts on their own servers, and some default settings keep them indefinitely. Agreeing to be recorded doesn't mean anyone agreed to that data sitting with a third party or being used for model training. Check your retention and training settings.
- **Transcript discoverability.** AI meeting transcripts are increasingly treated as discoverable in litigation. A sloppy, unreviewed AI summary can become evidence, with all the accuracy problems that implies.
- **Biometric data (BIPA).** Some note takers process voiceprints for speaker identification. Illinois's Biometric Information Privacy Act (740 ILCS 14) requires written notice and a written release before collecting biometric identifiers. That's a separate requirement from the recording question, and BIPA claims are part of the lawsuits above.
- **Hallucination and inaccuracy risk.** I've had AI note takers attribute a comment to the wrong speaker and, once, invent a numeric figure that was never said in the meeting. If that transcript gets treated as an authoritative record in a dispute, an HR complaint, or litigation, an uncorrected error is a real problem. Have someone check the transcript against their memory before treating it as ground truth.

## The Takeaway

The legal answer to "do I need consent for an AI note taker" depends on your state, and the law is still shifting as courts decide whether the vendor counts as a separate eavesdropper. The practical answer doesn't have to be complicated. Disclose the tool by name before you record, get a clear yes from everyone, and default to all-party consent whenever you're not certain who's in the room.

That one habit, a 15-second announcement at the start of every call, makes the state-by-state patchwork mostly irrelevant to your day-to-day.
